Why DSCR Binds Before LTV When Rates Rise

Worked example from the DSCR Debt Sizing Model example case · 48 units · $6.4M price

Ask a borrower how much a lender will give them and the first answer is almost always a loan-to-value number. "They'll go to 70 percent." It was a reasonable shorthand for most of the 2010s, when interest rates were low enough that the LTV cap was usually the constraint that mattered. It is a poor shorthand today. When rates rise, the debt service coverage ratio takes over long before the LTV cap is reached, and the loan proceeds a property supports fall with every basis point. This article shows exactly why, with numbers you can reproduce.

Three constraints, one answer

A commercial real estate lender sizes a loan three ways and takes the smallest result:

  • Loan-to-value (LTV): value × maximum LTV. Depends on price (or appraised value) and nothing else.
  • Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR): the loan whose annual debt service equals NOI ÷ minimum DSCR. Depends on NOI, the interest rate and the amortization term.
  • Debt yield: NOI ÷ minimum debt yield. Depends on NOI only.

Only one of the three moves when rates move: DSCR. That is the entire story. The LTV loan is fixed by the price, the debt yield loan is fixed by the NOI, and the DSCR loan shrinks as the cost of servicing each borrowed dollar rises.

The example case

The example property in the debt sizing template is a 48-unit apartment building bought for $6,400,000. After vacancy, concessions, eight expense lines, a 3 percent management fee and $300 per unit in reserves, stabilized NOI is $382,764. The going-in cap rate is 5.98 percent. The lender's box is a 70 percent maximum LTV, a 1.25x minimum DSCR tested on a 30-year amortizing payment, and an 8.5 percent minimum debt yield. The quoted rate is 6.75 percent fixed.

The DSCR loan is $546,000 below the LTV loan. The lender rounds down to $3,934,000, which works out to a 61.5 percent LTV and a 9.73 percent debt yield. The borrower who walked in expecting 70 percent leverage has to find an extra half-million of equity, and the reason is arithmetic, not the lender's mood.

Where the DSCR loan comes from

The DSCR constraint is easiest to understand through the loan constant: the annual debt service per dollar borrowed on an amortizing basis. At 6.75 percent over 30 years the constant is 0.07783. So:

DSCR loan = NOI ÷ (minimum DSCR × loan constant)
= $382,764 ÷ (1.25 × 0.07783)
= $3,934,265

Every input in that formula is either fixed by the property (NOI) or fixed by the lender (1.25x), except the loan constant, which is a function of the rate. Raise the rate, raise the constant, shrink the loan. The relationship is not linear but it is close to it over the range that matters.

The crossover rate

There is a specific rate at which the DSCR loan equals the LTV loan. Solving the DSCR formula for the loan constant that produces $4,480,000 gives 0.06835, which on a 30-year amortization corresponds to a rate of roughly 5.53 percent. Below that, LTV binds and the borrower gets their 70 percent. Above it, DSCR binds and every further increase comes straight out of proceeds. On this property, the market crossed that line a long time ago.

The crossover depends on the cap rate as much as the interest rate. A property bought at a 5.98 percent cap, like this one, has thin NOI relative to its price, so the DSCR loan is small relative to the LTV loan. The same lender box on a property bought at a 7.5 percent cap would see LTV bind at a much higher interest rate, because there is more NOI per dollar of price to cover the debt service. The general rule: when the cap rate is below the loan constant divided by the minimum DSCR times the maximum LTV, DSCR binds. Here that threshold is 0.07783 × 1.25 × 0.70 = 6.81 percent, and the property's 5.98 percent cap rate is comfortably below it.

Why NOI growth only partly helps

The other axis of the grid is NOI. Reading across the 6.75 percent row, a 10 percent increase in NOI lifts the loan to $4,327,000, and 15 percent is needed before the LTV cap of $4,480,000 finally binds. A 10 percent decrease drops it to $3,540,000. NOI moves the loan roughly one-for-one in percentage terms, which is useful to know when a seller's pro forma is 10 percent above the trailing twelve months: that gap is worth about $390,000 of proceeds, and the lender is going to underwrite the trailing number.

What this means for a borrower

  • Quote the DSCR loan, not the LTV loan, in your equity raise. If you model 70 percent leverage and the lender sizes at 61.5 percent, you are short $546,000 of equity at closing.
  • Negotiate the inputs that move the DSCR loan. A longer amortization lowers the constant. A lower minimum DSCR (1.20x instead of 1.25x) adds about 4 percent to proceeds. Underwritten NOI is worth arguing over line by line.
  • Do not expect an interest-only period to help. Most lenders test DSCR on the amortizing payment even during the IO period, which is why the template sizes that way. The IO period improves year-one cash flow, not loan size.
  • Re-run the sizing when the rate lock moves. A 50 basis point move between application and closing is a $194,000 change in proceeds on this deal. The dashboard in the template reports that number directly.

Checking your own deal

The whole analysis takes four numbers: NOI, price, the lender's three limits and the rate. Compute the loan constant for the quoted rate and amortization, divide NOI by the minimum DSCR times the constant, and compare the result with value times maximum LTV. If the DSCR figure is lower, you are in a DSCR-bound market for that asset, and the right question to ask a lender is not "what LTV will you go to" but "what rate and amortization are you sizing on."
