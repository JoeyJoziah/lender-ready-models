Equity Waterfall with IRR Hurdles and Promote: A Worked Example

Worked example from the Equity Waterfall Model example case · $3,000,000 equity · 10-year hold

Most descriptions of a distribution waterfall stop at the term sheet: "8 percent preferred return, then 80/20 to a 12, then 70/30 to a 15, then 60/40." That tells you the splits, not when each one starts, how much cash flows through each tier, or what the promote costs the investor. This article walks through the mechanics tier by tier on the example case in the waterfall template.

The deal

A joint venture buys a 120-unit value-add property with $3,000,000 of equity. The limited partner contributes 90 percent ($2,700,000) and the general partner co-invests 10 percent ($300,000). Distributable cash flow to equity, after debt service and before any promote, is:

The waterfall has four tiers. Tier 1 returns capital plus an 8 percent preferred return, split pro rata to capital (90/10). Tier 2 pays 80/20 until equity has earned a 12 percent IRR. Tier 3 pays 70/30 to a 15 percent IRR. Tier 4 splits anything above that 60/40.

How an IRR hurdle is tracked

The method used in most joint venture agreements, and in the template, is a hypothetical capital account per tier. For each tier, the account starts at the equity contributed, accrues at the hurdle rate each year, and is reduced by every distribution made to date, whether from this tier or an earlier one. Cash in a tier is paid out until that tier's account reaches zero. When it does, equity has earned exactly the hurdle IRR, and the remaining cash moves to the next tier.

Balance before distribution = beginning balance × (1 + hurdle) + contributions − distributions already made this year in earlier tiers
Tier distribution = MIN(balance before distribution, cash remaining)
Ending balance = balance before distribution − tier distribution

This is a whole-fund, or European, structure: hurdles are measured on the full equity investment over its life.

Tier 1: return of capital and the 8 percent preferred return

The Tier 1 account begins at $3,000,000. In year 1 it accrues $240,000 at 8 percent, bringing the balance to $3,240,000; the year's $165,000 of cash is paid entirely in Tier 1 and the account ends at $3,075,000. The pattern repeats. Cash flow in years 1 to 9 never exceeds the 8 percent accrual, so the account grows every year even as distributions are made:

In year 10 the balance before distribution is $3,432,870 and cash available is $6,400,000. Tier 1 takes $3,432,870 and the account closes at zero. The investors have now received their capital back plus an 8 percent compounded return. Over the whole hold, Tier 1 distributes $5,537,870, split $4,984,083 to the LP and $553,787 to the GP. Cash remaining after Tier 1 in year 10 is $2,967,130.

Tier 2: 80/20 to a 12 percent IRR

The Tier 2 account runs in parallel from year 0 at 12 percent, reduced each year by the Tier 1 distributions. By the start of year 10 it stands at $5,040,721; it accrues $604,887 and is reduced by the $3,432,870 paid in Tier 1 that year, leaving a balance of $2,212,738. Cash remaining is $2,967,130, so Tier 2 is paid in full: $2,212,738, of which $1,770,190 goes to the LP and $442,548 to the GP. Equity has now earned a 12 percent IRR, and $754,392 remains.

Tier 3: 70/30 to a 15 percent IRR

The Tier 3 account at 15 percent, after the same deductions, has a balance before distribution of $2,260,278 in year 10. Only $754,392 remains, so Tier 3 takes all of it and the account ends at $1,505,886, still positive. Equity did not reach a 15 percent IRR, so Tier 4 receives nothing. The Tier 3 cash splits $528,074 to the LP and $226,318 to the GP.

What the promote is worth

With no promote, the GP's 10 percent co-invest would have earned a pro rata 10 percent of all distributions: $850,500. It actually received $1,222,652. The promote is the difference, $372,152, or 6.76 percent of total project profit. Expressed the way an LP will think about it: the project IRR of 13.09 percent is what the LP would have earned with no promote; the LP actually earns 12.51 percent, a give-up of 58 basis points. That is the price of the sponsor's work and alignment, and it is worth knowing before signing an operating agreement.

Note where the promote came from. The deal cleared the 8 and 12 percent hurdles but not the 15, so the GP's promote was earned in Tiers 2 and 3 only, and the 60/40 residual tier never came into play. A sponsor's deck that projects returns in Tier 4 is projecting an outcome above a 15 percent whole-fund IRR; it is worth asking what exit value that assumes.

Sensitivity to exit

Because almost all of the cash arrives in year 10, the result is sensitive to the sale price. Scaling the final-year cash flow by 10 percent either way moves the project IRR from 13.09 percent to 12.17 percent or 13.94 percent, and the multiple from 2.835x to 2.622x or 3.048x. At a 70 percent exit, the project IRR is 10.07 percent and the deal clears Tier 1 but barely reaches into Tier 2; at 130 percent it is 15.47 percent and Tier 4 begins to pay. The promote is highly convex in the exit, which is the structural reason sponsors and investors argue about exit cap rates.

Reading a waterfall in an operating agreement

  • Confirm the lookback basis. Whole-fund IRR hurdles, as here, are the norm in JVs. Deal-by-deal or annual preferred returns without a lookback behave differently.
  • Check whether the preferred return compounds. The example compounds annually. A simple (non-compounding) pref produces a lower Tier 1 balance and moves cash into the promoted tiers sooner.
  • Look for a catch-up. Some agreements let the GP take 100 percent of cash after the pref until it has received a fixed share of total profit. The example has none; a catch-up shifts more cash to the GP at the same project return.
  • Tie the splits to the capital accounts, not the headline. "80/20 above an 8" means nothing until you know how the 8 is measured and when it is deemed reached.
