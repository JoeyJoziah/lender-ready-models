Debt Yield vs DSCR vs LTV: How Lenders Size a Loan

Worked example from the DSCR Debt Sizing Model example case · stabilized multifamily · $382,764 NOI

A lender's term sheet usually lists three limits: a maximum loan-to-value, a minimum debt service coverage ratio and a minimum debt yield. Borrowers tend to focus on whichever one they understand best and treat the other two as boilerplate. They are not boilerplate. Each limit protects the lender against a different thing going wrong, each one responds to different inputs, and the loan you actually get is the smallest of the three. This article explains what each ratio measures, why a lender wants all three, and walks through a sizing on a real example case.

Loan-to-value: protection against a fall in price

LTV loan = value × maximum LTV

LTV is the oldest and simplest test. If the lender advances 70 percent of value, the property has to lose 30 percent of its value before the lender loses a dollar in a foreclosure sale. Value is normally the lower of the purchase price and the appraised value; on a refinance it is the appraisal alone. The test says nothing about whether the property can pay its mortgage. A building with no tenants and a generous appraisal passes LTV comfortably.

That is its weakness. When prices rise faster than income, LTV lets leverage run ahead of the cash flow that has to service it, which is why the other two tests carry more weight now.

DSCR: protection against the property not paying

DSCR = NOI ÷ annual debt service
DSCR loan = NOI ÷ (minimum DSCR × loan constant)

Debt service coverage asks whether the net operating income covers the mortgage payment with room to spare. A 1.25x minimum means NOI must be at least 125 percent of annual debt service, leaving a 20 percent cushion for income to fall before the property can no longer pay. The loan constant (annual payment per dollar borrowed, including principal) converts that into a loan amount. Two conventions matter: most lenders test on the amortizing payment even if the loan has an interest-only period, and the rate used is the note rate for fixed-rate loans or a stressed rate for floating-rate loans.

DSCR is the only one of the three tests that responds to interest rates, because the loan constant rises with the rate. That makes it the constraint that usually binds when rates are high relative to cap rates.

Debt yield: protection against both, independent of rates

Debt yield = NOI ÷ loan amount
Debt yield loan = NOI ÷ minimum debt yield

Debt yield is the newest of the three in common use and the one borrowers understand least. It is the return the lender would earn on its loan balance if it owned the property outright, which is to say the cap rate at which the loan amount equals the value. An 8.5 percent minimum debt yield means the lender will not advance more than NOI divided by 8.5 percent regardless of the interest rate, the amortization, or the appraised value.

The point of the test is that it cannot be gamed. A low interest rate or a long amortization lifts the DSCR loan; a generous appraisal lifts the LTV loan; nothing lifts the debt yield loan except more NOI. Lenders use it as a floor on the quality of the loan through a full cycle, which is why it tends to be the binding constraint when rates are unusually low and the DSCR test is loose.

The three side by side

The example case in the debt sizing template is a 48-unit property with NOI of $382,764, bought for $6,400,000, financed at 6.75 percent over 30 years. The lender's limits are 70 percent LTV, 1.25x DSCR and 8.5 percent debt yield.

LTV$6,400,000 × 70%$4,480,000
DSCR$382,764 ÷ (1.25 × 0.07783)$3,934,265
Debt yield$382,764 ÷ 8.5%$4,503,100

DSCR binds. The loan is rounded down to $3,934,000, and the template then reports the resulting metrics on that loan: a 61.5 percent LTV, a 1.25x DSCR on the amortizing payment, a 1.44x DSCR on the interest-only payment during the IO period, and a 9.73 percent debt yield. The lender gets a cushion on all three tests even though only one was the limit.

Which one binds, and when

A useful way to see the relationship is to express all three as a cap rate on the loan amount. The LTV loan is binding when the property's cap rate is high relative to the loan constant; the DSCR loan is binding when the loan constant is high relative to the cap rate; the debt yield loan is binding when the loan constant is low relative to the required yield. In the example:

  • At 6.75 percent, the loan constant is 7.78 percent and 1.25x coverage requires NOI of 9.73 percent of the loan. The debt yield floor of 8.5 percent is weaker than that, so DSCR binds.
  • If the rate fell to about 5.5 percent, the DSCR loan would rise to meet the LTV loan at $4,480,000, and at that loan the debt yield is 8.54 percent, barely above the 8.5 percent floor. A fraction lower in rate and the debt yield test takes over from both.
  • If NOI rises 15 percent with the rate unchanged, the DSCR loan reaches $4,480,000 and LTV binds. The template's sensitivity grid shows this: at +15 percent NOI, every rate from 6.0 to 6.75 percent returns the LTV cap.

So the three tests hand off to each other as conditions change. Rising rates push DSCR to the front. Very low rates make DSCR generous and bring debt yield forward. Lenders carry all three because no single one is reliable in every market.

Reading a term sheet

Do the three calculations yourself before responding to a quote. Things to check:

  1. Which NOI is the lender using? Underwritten NOI is usually below the seller's pro forma: a vacancy floor (often 5 percent even if actual is lower), a management fee at a market rate, and reserves of $250 to $300 per unit on multifamily are standard adjustments. Each of them reduces the DSCR and debt yield loans but not the LTV loan.
  2. Which value is the lender using? On an acquisition, the lower of price and appraisal. If you paid above appraisal, the LTV loan shrinks.
  3. What rate is the DSCR sized on? For floating-rate debt, lenders typically size on a stressed rate above the current index, which lowers the DSCR loan relative to the spot rate.
  4. Is the DSCR tested on the amortizing or interest-only payment? Amortizing is the norm. A lender that tests on the IO payment is offering materially more leverage, and will usually be charging for it elsewhere.

What to negotiate

The negotiation depends on which constraint binds. If DSCR binds, the levers are amortization (35 years instead of 30 lowers the constant), the minimum ratio (1.20x versus 1.25x adds about 4 percent to proceeds) and the underwritten NOI. If LTV binds, the lever is the appraisal or the lender's maximum. If debt yield binds, nothing helps except more NOI or a lender with a lower floor, because the test ignores everything else.
