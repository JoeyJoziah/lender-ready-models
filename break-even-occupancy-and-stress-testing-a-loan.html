Break-Even Occupancy and Stress Testing a Loan

Worked example from the DSCR Debt Sizing Model example case · 48 units · $3,934,000 loan

Sizing a loan tells you how much a lender will advance on today's numbers. Stress testing tells you how much room there is before today's numbers stop working. Lenders do this as a matter of routine; borrowers should do it before they sign, because the equity is the first thing lost when the cushion runs out. This article covers the two tools that matter most, break-even occupancy and a rate-and-NOI sensitivity grid, using the example case from the debt sizing template.

The base case

The property is a 48-unit apartment building bought for $6,400,000 with stabilized NOI of $382,764. The lender sizes a $3,934,000 loan at 6.75 percent, 30-year amortization, with 12 months of interest-only. Annual debt service on the amortizing payment is $306,190, giving a 1.25x DSCR, which is exactly the lender's minimum. Total operating expenses are $425,652, gross potential rent is $835,200 and other income is $31,680. The cushion above debt service is $76,573 a year once the loan amortizes.

Break-even occupancy

Break-even occupancy is the occupancy rate at which income exactly covers operating expenses plus debt service. Below it, the property cannot pay its mortgage from operations and the owner is funding the shortfall. The template computes it as:

Break-even occupancy = (operating expenses + annual debt service − other income) ÷ gross potential rent
= ($425,652 + $306,190 − $31,680) ÷ $835,200
= 83.8%

The property is underwritten at 94 percent economic occupancy (a 6 percent vacancy and credit loss allowance). It can lose about ten points of occupancy before it stops covering debt service. Whether ten points is a lot depends on the market and the asset: for stabilized suburban multifamily it is a reasonable cushion; for a property with the lease rollover concentration in a bad submarket, or for an office building, it is thin.

Two refinements are worth knowing. The template's figure holds the management fee at the base-case dollar amount, which slightly overstates break-even since the fee would fall with income; the difference here is under half a point. And break-even on the interest-only payment is lower (about 79 percent on this deal, since debt service drops to $265,545) but it is the amortizing number that matters, because that is the payment the property has to carry for the other nine years of the term.

Why a single DSCR is not enough

A 1.25x DSCR means NOI can fall 20 percent before coverage reaches 1.0x. That is the cushion from the lender's seat. From the borrower's seat the relevant questions are different: what happens to the loan amount if rates move before closing, what happens to coverage if the pro forma NOI is not achieved, and how much of each can the deal absorb at once. The answer requires a grid, not a ratio.

This grid answers the pre-closing question. If the rate lock slips 50 basis points, the loan drops to $3,740,000, a $194,000 equity gap. If the lender's underwriter cuts NOI by 10 percent against the pro forma, which is common when a vacancy floor and market management fee are applied, the loan drops to $3,540,000. Both at once, 7.25 percent and −10 percent, gives $3,366,000: $568,000 less than the base case, and the borrower is bringing 14 percent more equity than planned.

Stress grid two: DSCR at the base loan

The second grid holds the loan at $3,934,000 and recomputes coverage. This is the post-closing question: once the debt is in place, how far can things go wrong before the property fails its covenant or cannot pay.

For a fixed-rate loan the rate rows matter at refinance, not during the term. The NOI columns matter every month. A 10 percent NOI shortfall takes coverage to 1.13x, below the 1.25x the lender required at closing and likely below any cash-sweep or covenant threshold in the loan documents. A 15 percent shortfall combined with a refinance at 7.5 percent takes coverage below 1.0x, and the property no longer pays its own debt service.

For a floating-rate loan the rate rows matter immediately, which is why lenders size floating-rate debt on a stressed rate rather than the current index and why rate caps are not optional.

Cash-on-cash under stress

Equity returns are more sensitive than coverage, because debt service is a fixed charge. In the base case, year-one cash flow after the interest-only debt service of $265,545 is $117,219, a 4.21 percent cash-on-cash return on $2,783,340 of equity. Once the loan amortizes, cash flow after debt service falls to $76,573 and cash-on-cash to 2.75 percent. A 10 percent NOI shortfall, $38,276, takes the amortizing cash flow to about $38,000 and the return to about 1.4 percent. A 20 percent shortfall takes it to zero. Leverage that produces a 1.25x DSCR produces an equity return that is almost entirely dependent on hitting the pro forma.

A stress-testing routine

  1. Compute break-even occupancy on the amortizing payment and compare it with the worst trailing twelve-month occupancy the property or submarket has recorded. If the worst year was below break-even, the deal needs less leverage or more reserves.
  2. Size the loan at the rate lock plus 50 basis points and carry the lower number in the equity raise. Treat any difference at closing as a pleasant surprise.
  3. Size the loan at underwritten NOI, not pro forma. Apply a 5 percent vacancy floor, a market management fee and $250 to $300 per unit of reserves yourself before the lender does.
  4. Find the cell where DSCR crosses 1.0x and ask whether that combination is plausible over the loan term. On this deal it is −15 percent NOI at 7.5 percent, which is a refinance-risk scenario rather than an operating one, but it exists.
  5. Hold a reserve sized to the gap. If a 10 percent NOI shortfall costs $38,000 a year of cash flow, a $100,000 operating reserve buys roughly two and a half years of cover.
